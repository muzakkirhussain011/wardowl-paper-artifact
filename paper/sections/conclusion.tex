Permission-aware retrieval is usually described as a filter. We have argued that it is better
treated as a composition of untrusted parts, translators, approximate indexes and authorization
engines, around one trusted part, the check API. Re-checking every hit with that API makes the
composition secure whatever the other parts do, and our measurements bear this out: no leak in any
store or engine configuration we tested, including under red-team prompts.

Security, however, is the easy half. The other parts still decide how much of what a person may read
they actually receive, and there the stack is far from faithful. Translators disagree with the checks
they stand for, stores drop matching documents at size limits, and filtered approximate search loses
most of a readable set when that set is small or far from the query, all without any signal to the
application. These are not exotic configurations: they arise from documented defaults and from the
ordinary shape of organizational permissions. \tbd{one sentence with the X2 and X5 headline numbers}

Three practices follow. Test filter semantics differentially, against a reference that shares no code
with the translators. Measure recall under permission-shaped filters against exact search over the
readable set, on measured correlation axes, before choosing a store or a layout. And never let a
system call a short approximate answer complete: count, and fall back to exact search within a bound,
or say that results may be missing. We release the kit, the harnesses and the data so that these
checks can be repeated as stores and engines change.
