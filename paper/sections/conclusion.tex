Permission-aware retrieval is usually described as a filter. We have argued that it is better treated as a composition of untrusted
parts, translators, approximate indexes and filter APIs, around a small trusted computing base centered on the check API. Re-checking
every hit makes containment independent of the untrusted parts, and our measurements agree: no leak in any of eleven end-to-end
configurations (one-sided 95\% bounds of $6.8 \times 10^{-4}$ per question and $1.6 \times 10^{-2}$ per person, over the question
generator's distribution), and none in 1,050 fault episodes ($2.8 \times 10^{-3}$ per episode, over the faults we injected). When we broke
the filter on purpose, the leak checks failed as they should and the re-check contained the fault.

Containment, however, is the easy part, and it is not all that security asks. The checked revision can be stale. Latency, counts, recall
and scores told an attacker when unreadable chunks lay near a query. And the untrusted parts still decide how much of what a subject may
read it receives: stores dropped matching chunks at size limits, filtered approximate search lost much of a readable set far from the
question, and the same pgvector configuration was exact in some runs and lost about a quarter of its recall in others. A filter-only count
flagged every truly short answer without a false alarm and an exact search without a bound repaired them all, but no count sees a full answer
of the wrong chunks.

Three practices follow. Test filter semantics differentially, and measure recall under permission-shaped filters against exact search over the
readable set before choosing a store, a layout or a plan. Never let a system call a short approximate answer complete: count, fall back to exact
search within a bound, and sample exact searches to see what a count cannot. Treat the shape and timing of an answer as outputs too, and the
staleness of a check as part of the claim. We release the kit, the harnesses and the data so that these checks can be repeated as stores and
engines change.
