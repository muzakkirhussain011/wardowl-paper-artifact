\section{Filter size in detail (X3)}\label{sec:qual-size}

This appendix reports the filter-size experiment, which Section~\ref{sec:qual-frontiers} summarizes in a paragraph.

A person in many directory groups turns the permission filter into a long disjunction: the filter of
Appendix~\ref{sec:sys-contracts} holds one value per group, and enterprise users can belong to
thousands. We vary only that length. Each of five seeds builds a corpus of 100,000 vectors of 384
dimensions, the 57,638 FiQA passages embedded as in Appendix~\ref{sec:side-setup} and 42,362 synthetic
vectors around 200 seeded cluster centers, and makes a fixed 10\% of it readable at every level: a
readable document holds one group of each level, and the subject at level $G$ holds exactly that
level's $G$ groups, for $G$ from 1 to 40,000. The readable set, and so the exact answer, is the same
at every level; only the filter grows. Twelve stores run at default index settings, with FAISS
over a flat index and the in-memory backend as controls, and each level is timed over 30 questions
(10 when the level's first retrieval takes more than 2\,s) with $k = 10$ in three passes after a
warm-up, a retrieval being allowed 120\,s. We record whether
each store's translator returns and how fast, whether the store accepts the filter or refuses it,
and with which error, the latency percentiles, and recall@10 against exact search over the readable
set; for pgvector also the plan. The limits the stores document that could bind are a 65,536-term
cap per \texttt{terms} query in Elasticsearch and OpenSearch, PostgreSQL's 65,535 bind parameters,
SQLite's 32,766 host parameters and expression depth of 1,000 under Chroma, and the request sizes of
MongoDB (16\,MB) and Qdrant (32\,MB); the layer's Cassandra adapter falls back to one unfiltered
query filtered locally beyond 254 groups, as documented.

We pre-registered that every translator returns within a second at every level; that at 40,000
groups Elasticsearch, OpenSearch, Qdrant, pgvector, LanceDB, Cassandra, FAISS and the in-memory
backend accept the filter and Chroma refuses it; that pgvector's median latency grows at least
fiftyfold from one group to 40,000, because above 100 values the layer sends an array test that the
GIN index cannot serve, while the controls' grows at most twofold; and that recall does not depend
on the filter's length in any approximate store that accepts it (paired difference within
$\pm 0.02$).

Table~\ref{tab:qual-size} shows the result. Every translator returned at every level, the slowest
in a median 197\,ms (Cassandra's fallback with 40,000 groups) and 120\,ms (pgvector), and every store
but Chroma accepted every level in every seed without one failed retrieval, the five stores without a
prediction included; no documented limit was reached. Acceptance with 40,000 groups was confirmed for
each of the eight stores we predicted (X3-H2). In the main run Chroma completed only three of the five
seeds the rules require: each of its seeds took 22 minutes, and the harness, which gave each store of
a job an equal share of the job's time, skipped the last two although the job then finished about
three hours early. We ran those two seeds afterwards, exactly as written, on the same data and in a
job of their own, under an amendment specified in writing after the main results and before running;
recomputed with them, every other store's tables came out unchanged. With five seeds, X3-H1 holds in
all 84 of its parts.

Latency grew as predicted where we predicted it (X3-H3 confirmed in all three parts). pgvector's
median rose 272-fold (95\% interval 264 to 288) from one group to 40,000: by a factor of 1.6 up to
100 groups, then roughly in proportion to $G$. The plans show why, and that the reason we
pre-registered did not decide it. At every level and in every seed the planner chose the same HNSW
index scan, never the GIN index, not even for one group, and the scan removed the same rows through
the filter at every level (55 to 142 for each seed's first question); only the cost of testing each
row grew, so that question took 1.1 to 2.6\,ms with one group and 239 to 566\,ms with 40,000. Its
answers did not change either: recall@10 was 0.967 at every level, and the paired difference between
40,000 groups and one was 0.000 (0.000 to 0.000). FAISS and the in-memory backend slowed 1.19 and 1.17 times, and Cassandra's
102 per-group queries with 100 groups took 1.85 times as long (1.82 to 1.87) as its single fallback
query with 1,000. The other eight servers slowed 11 to 99 times, mostly beyond 1,000 groups, and with
40,000 groups the translation alone took 31 to 48\,ms of medians between 72 and 306\,ms: much of the
cost of a long filter lies in building and shipping it. LanceDB, which scans exactly, slowed 7.94
times.

Recall moved by at most 0.012 with the filter's length in the ten approximate stores that accepted every level
(X3-H4 confirmed for eight of the nine stores it names, inconclusive for Chroma; pgvector's paired difference was 0.000, and Cassandra's is below). The paired difference between 40,000 groups and one was 0.000,
with an interval of 0.000 to 0.000, for Qdrant, Elasticsearch, OpenSearch, Weaviate, MongoDB and
Vespa, 0.000 ($-0.0007$ to 0.0007) for Redis, and 0.012 (0.006 to 0.019) for Milvus, whose recall rose
from 0.987 to 0.999 with the longer filter, within the pre-registered band; Cassandra's fallback,
exploratory, gained 0.004 (0.0007 to 0.0093).

Chroma refused, as predicted, but in two ways. With 1,000 groups or more, every retrieval failed with
SQLite's ``Expression tree is too large (maximum depth 1000)'', which Chroma raises as an
\texttt{InternalError}: because the layer stores list values as boolean keys
(Table~\ref{tab:conf-faults}), its disjunction holds one clause per group, 102 with 100 groups and
1,002 with 1,000, and SQLite limits an expression's depth to 1,000. With 40,000 groups the process
died with signal 11, a segmentation fault, during its first retrieval, in all five seeds as in both
smoke runs. The layer's translation had succeeded (2.66 million characters in 101\,ms), no message was
printed, and no error reached the layer: the application's process ends with the store's. A filter
that kills the client process is a denial of service, not a refusal. The amendment's exploratory
part, likewise specified in writing after the main results and before running,
located the crash. Python's fault handler placed it in Chroma's call into its native core, and under gdb the main
thread was about 16,500 frames deep in a single recursive function of Chroma's Rust bindings when its
stack ran out, before SQLite saw the query. One retrieval per level, each in a fresh process, put the
threshold between 15,500 groups, refused with SQLite's error, and 16,500, which crashed, on runners with a 16\,MiB main-thread stack limit (another limit would move it, which we did not test), consistent
with one stack frame per clause of the disjunction. Chroma renders an $n$-ary
disjunction as a left-deep chain of binary ORs, so the depth grows with the number of clauses; the
layer now nests its disjunctions sixteen clauses to a node, which keeps the depth near 50 for 1,000
groups, and never sends a filter beyond conservative bounds on parameters, depth, nesting and size,
filtering 1,000 candidates itself instead, as its Cassandra adapter already did; we have not evaluated
what those 1,000 candidates cost in recall. In the CI of the pull request that made the change
(\url{https://github.com/muzakkirhussain011/wardowl/pull/187}, merged on 2026-10-30 with its checks
green, not yet in a release), the fixed layer retrieved exactly the permitted chunks from real Chroma
for subjects in 1,001 and 40,000 groups. We reported both behaviors to Chroma on 2026-10-30, after that pull request was public (issues \#7969 and \#7970; Section~\ref{sec:limits-ethics}). Chroma was also the slowest store at every level
it accepted, 4.3\,s with one group and 10.6\,s with 100, and each refused retrieval still took about
4\,s. With five seeds, its X3-H2 verdict is confirmed; X3-H4 stays inconclusive for Chroma, as the
rule needs an accepted level of at least 1,000 groups.
