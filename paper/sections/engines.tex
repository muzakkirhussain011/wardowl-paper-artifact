Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction} lets a pushed-down filter be wrong without a leak, but not without
cost: a false denial silently loses recall, and a false admit becomes a violation. We ask whether the
filters \sys{} compiles from four engines admit exactly what the same engines' checks allow,
separating three causes of disagreement: a translation fault, which persists at one revision;
revision skew, a write between the two calls; and truncated list APIs. We then measure what items
(iii) and (v) of the threat model (Section~\ref{sec:prob-threat}) require: how long a revocation
keeps admitting, and whether the layer fails closed when the authorizer or the store fails. Hypotheses, rules and
statistics were pre-registered; we report every verdict, falsified ones included.

\subsection{Method}\label{sec:eng-method}

We tested OpenFGA 1.22.0 and SpiceDB 1.56.2 on a PostgreSQL datastore, OPA 1.21.2 and Cerbos 0.56.0
through the adapters of Section~\ref{sec:system}, in GitHub-hosted runners. For each of five seeds a
generator draws 3 tenants, 120 users, 60 groups and 80 folders (both nested up to four levels) and
1,500 documents with owners, direct readers, reader groups, occasional everyone-readable grants,
bans of users and groups, and a classification. A user may read a document of their own tenant that
they own or that is shared with them directly, through a group, with everyone or through a folder,
unless banned; engines that see attributes add a clearance condition. OpenFGA and SpiceDB hold this
model as relationships (tenant intersection, \texttt{but not banned}, inheritance through parent
folders, a \texttt{user:*} wildcard), OpenFGA once with stored group memberships and once with
memberships sent as contextual tuples. OPA holds it as a filter document over ACL metadata, one over
sharing data, and its own partial evaluation~\cite{opa2026evaluating,opa2026writing},
whose residual we translate (equality and membership to $\mathrm{In}$, conjunction and disjunction
as such, anything else to no filter). Cerbos derives filters from query plans~\cite{cerbos2026cerbos}.
OPA and Cerbos omit bans here, because the filter language (Section~\ref{sec:sys-contracts}) has no
negation; stress profiles add bans and one-value ACL fields stored without a list. An independent
Python oracle evaluates the model, and two controls (filter and check both the oracle; a filter with
2\% planted errors) validate the counting.

\emph{One revision} means SpiceDB's \texttt{atExactSnapshot} at the token of the last
write~\cite{pang2019zanzibar,authzed2026consistency}; OpenFGA offers no snapshot token, so its store
was read quiescent with \texttt{HIGHER\_CONSISTENCY}; OPA and Cerbos are stateless per request. For
every user and document we record a \emph{false admit} (the filter admits, the check denies) or a
\emph{false denial}, and repeat each disagreement twice. Skew is measured under a writer committing
20 toggles a second, half about the user being measured; a disagreement counts as skew when the
oracle's answer changes between the last write visible to the filter (allowing 10\,s of staleness for
OpenFGA's \texttt{MINIMIZE\_LATENCY} and 5.5\,s for SpiceDB's \texttt{minimizeLatency}) and the end
of the check. Truncation is measured in a corpus of 100,000 documents, growing one subject's readable
set from 10 to 100,000, with 30 queries per level and seed over exact FAISS search and the layer's
defaults ($k = 10$, at most 1,000 candidates). Intervals are 95\% cluster bootstraps over (seed,
user) or (seed, query); after zero events among $n$ clusters we give the bound $1 - 0.05^{1/n}$.

Table~\ref{tab:eng-agree} shows no translation fault. In the seven profiles that hold the model as
intended, 6.3 million pairs produced no disagreement between filter and check, and every check agreed
with the oracle (one-sided 95\% bound on users with any disagreement: 0.5\% per profile). The
oracle control reported nothing and the planted control exactly its 16,774 false admits and 1,233
false denials. The only disagreement at one revision is the data-shape gap of
Section~\ref{sec:conf-campaign}: when 10\% of documents store a one-value ACL list as a plain value,
Cerbos's plan admits what its check denies in 569 pairs (0.063\% of pairs; 52.5\% of users, 95\%
interval 48.3\% to 56.3\%), always on such a document and reproducibly; the re-check turns each
into a violation. OPA's policy, which reads a field as one value or a list, stays exact on the same
data. Neither translator dropped a negation: with bans, Cerbos's plan and OPA's
residual both kept a \texttt{not}, and no user received a filter. For OPA this falsified our
prediction that partial evaluation would simplify \texttt{not (x in [])} away for the 73 users with no
ban in scope; it does not, so one ban rule makes OPA's partially evaluated filter unavailable to
everyone, who are then served by post-filtering.

\subsection{In practice, disagreement is revision skew}\label{sec:eng-skew}

Unpinned under the writer, filter and check disagreed in every OpenFGA trial with either consistency
level (9,433 and 9,972 pairs over 200 trials each), in 81.5\% of SpiceDB trials with
\texttt{fullyConsistent} (95\% interval 76.0\% to 86.5\%), and in 13.0\% of OPA trials (8.5\% to
18.0\%), whose calls are a few milliseconds apart. A write between the calls explained every
disagreeing pair, and none reproduced. Pinned to one SpiceDB snapshot under the same writer, 200
trials produced no disagreement; neither did the quiet conditions. SpiceDB's default,
\texttt{minimizeLatency}, disagreed in only 4.0\% of trials (1.5\% to 7.0\%), not because it is
fresher but because both calls read the same quantized, possibly stale snapshot; 226 of its 228 pairs
were explained, and the other two are consistent with staleness beyond the 5.5\,s allowed, which
Section~\ref{sec:eng-revoke} measures. The attribution is generous where windows hold dozens of writes (medians of 59 and 208 for
OpenFGA), so the pinned and quiet conditions carry the claim: an online violation is evidence of a
translation fault only when filter and check read one revision (Section~\ref{sec:prob-criteria}).

\subsection{Truncated lists are detected, at a cost in recall}\label{sec:eng-trunc}

Non-streaming ListObjects and LookupResources with the layer's limit of 1,000 both returned exactly
$\min(N, 1000)$ objects; ListObjects adds no other field~\cite{openfga2026openfga} and
LookupResources attaches a continuation cursor whatever $N$~\cite{authzed2026protecting}, so neither
signals truncation. Without those limits both returned all 100,000 objects in time: streamed ListObjects in a median 1.7
to 2.0\,s, within its 3\,s deadline, and unlimited LookupResources in 2.6 to 3.7\,s. \sys{} returned
an exact filter for every $N \le 999$ and none for $N \ge 1000$ in all 540 calls, never a short one;
over 8,100 retrievals, no result with recall below 1 was marked complete and none leaked. The cost
lies at the limit. With 1,000 or 1,001 readable documents among 100,000, the post-filter's 1,000
candidates hold about ten readable ones, and recall@10 fell to 0.855 to 0.891 per level, against
1.000 with the filter below the limit and from 3,000 readable documents up. A list limit equal to the
post-filter's cap thus places the loss where selectivity is about 1\%; paging or streaming the list
would remove it. OPA, which has no list limit, gave exact filters at every size, but over a sharing
document of 100,000 entries its partial evaluation took a median 4.2 to 5.7\,s, past the adapters'
default 2\,s timeout, so every retrieval through it failed closed and its recall was not measured.

\subsection{Latency and caching}\label{sec:eng-latency}

An uncached filter took a median 0.9\,ms (OpenFGA, 10 objects, a model without intersection) to
324\,ms (OpenFGA's full model, 100,000 objects), 22 to 30\,ms for SpiceDB once its limit binds,
1.2\,ms for Cerbos, and 1.7 to 13.6\,ms for OPA's filter documents and 116\,ms for its partial
evaluation over 1,500 documents. A check took 0.13 to 1.4\,ms per hit on those worlds. Caching paid as
predicted for OpenFGA, SpiceDB and OPA, at least 17.5 times faster in every pre-registered cell.
Cerbos's check is so cheap that caching sped it up only 8.6 times (95\% interval 8.5 to 8.7): short
of the predicted factor of 10, but above the falsification bound of 5, so inconclusive.

\paragraph{Revocation.}
The cache's time to live is the realized delay, not merely a bound: every ttl median in
Table~\ref{tab:eng-revoke} lies within 34\,ms of 5\,s or 30\,s, and invalidation ends the delay at
once, or within the shared replica's 1\,s local copy. Permissions copied into chunk metadata were
revoked within milliseconds whatever the cache: the store filters current metadata, and cached
decisions are keyed by it. Two sources of truth were stale themselves.
Cerbos reloaded a rewritten policy file in a median 2.5\,s (at most 4.0\,s). SpiceDB under
\texttt{minimizeLatency}, \sys{}'s default, took a median 3.3\,s and at most 8.7\,s, beyond the
6.5\,s predicted from its quantization window~\cite{authzed2026consistency}, and in 28 polls a revoked
chunk reappeared after it had disappeared. Invalidation does not survive such an engine: the next call
refills the cache from the stale snapshot, which the cache holds for its full ttl, so 79 of 80 SpiceDB
revocations with invalidation exceeded their bound (medians 30.0 and 31.0\,s), and Cerbos's took as
long. After invalidation the bound is ttl plus staleness, not staleness; a cache that declines to
refill during the engine's staleness window would restore it, which we evaluate below.

\paragraph{Faults.}
We injected 14 faults into each engine, by a proxy or by killing the server, and five or eight into
pgvector and Qdrant, in 1,090 episodes of a 3\,s baseline, a 12\,s fault and recovery. No retrieval
returned a chunk outside the user's readable set: 0 leaks in 623,279 requests (at most
$4.8 \times 10^{-6}$ per request, one-sided 95\%), while a control that turned the engine's denials
into allows leaked in every engine. Every engine-caused failure was the layer's authorization error
with a message naming no host. Transport faults (server down, connections refused or never answered,
delays past the 2\,s timeout, HTTP 500 and 503) failed every request inside the fault, and recovery
took a median 0.02 to 0.18\,s after removal. A circuit breaker failed requests in under 0.1\,ms
instead of 2\,s, at the price of a median 3.1 to 3.6\,s to the first success after recovery; a warm
30\,s cache answered every request through the 12\,s outages. Answers altered in content failed
closed, except that an empty LookupResources stream reads as ``no readable documents'': 4,746
retrievals returned nothing without an error, losing recall only. Store faults surfaced as the
clients' own exceptions, unwrapped, some with the store's raw response, and pgvector's pool held
blocked requests for its 5\,s timeout or longer, so its unanswered-connection case put no request
inside the window.

\paragraph{The fixes, tested.}
The run exposed five defects in the layer, and we fixed all five. After an invalidation the cache
now caches nothing for the engine's declared staleness, and the SpiceDB adapter, whose documentation
had promised 5\,s, declares 11\,s without full consistency: twice the documented 5.5\,s, since we
measured 8.7\,s. An empty LookupResources stream now yields no filter, so the retriever post-filters
and checks every candidate. Every store failure is raised as one search error with a fixed message
and the client's exception as its cause. And pgvector can set a statement timeout. A targeted rerun, pre-registered
as an amendment before it ran, with requests started every 0.2\,s so that some are in flight when a
fault begins, confirmed all of its predictions. With invalidation, all 160 SpiceDB and Cerbos
revocations took effect within the predicted bound (the declared staleness plus 1\,s, and 1\,s more
on the replica): medians of
4.8\,s (at most 5.2\,s) for SpiceDB and 3.9\,s for Cerbos, against 30 and 31\,s before. SpiceDB itself
still flapped, serving a revoked chunk again after it had gone in 16 to 28 polls per condition; the
cache no longer prolongs that. Under the emptied stream, 3,520 retrievals failed closed instead of
returning nothing, and with only the lookups emptied 3,180 returned exactly the right answer through
post-filtering. All 6,066 store failures carried the fixed message and named no host. pgvector's
statement timeout turned a table lock into failures after a median 1.003\,s with no request hanging,
against 80 hanging requests and a median of 5\,s without it; for a connection that is accepted but never answered it cannot
help, since the server never sees the statement, and every request hung or failed after 5\,s with or
without it, as predicted. Only a deadline on the whole retrieval, which the layer does not yet offer,
would bound that case. In 10,303 requests under these faults no chunk leaked (one-sided 95\% bound
$2.9 \times 10^{-4}$ per request).
