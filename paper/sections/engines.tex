Observation~\ref{prop:reduction} lets a pushed-down filter be wrong without a leak, but not without
cost. We ask whether the filters \sys{} compiles from four engines admit exactly what the same engines'
checks allow, separating a translation fault, which persists at one revision, from revision skew, a write
between the two calls, and from truncated list APIs. We then measure how long a revocation keeps admitting
and whether the layer fails closed. We tested OpenFGA 1.22.0, SpiceDB 1.56.2, OPA 1.21.2 and Cerbos 0.56.0
on five seeds of a generated world of 3 tenants, 120 users, 60 groups, 80 folders and 1,500 documents,
against an independent oracle, with controls that validate the counting. \emph{One revision} means
SpiceDB's \texttt{atExactSnapshot} at the token of the last write; OpenFGA offers no snapshot token, so its
store was read quiescent; OPA and Cerbos are stateless per request (Appendix~\ref{app:engines}).

\paragraph{Agreement at one revision.}
In the seven profiles that hold the model as intended, 6.3 million (user, document) pairs produced no
disagreement between filter and check, and every check agreed with the oracle (one-sided 95\% bound on
users with any disagreement: 0.5\% per profile; X4-H1 confirmed; Table~\ref{tab:eng-agree} in
Appendix~\ref{sec:eng-agree}). The only disagreement at one revision is the Cerbos type gap: when 10\% of
documents store a one-value ACL list as a plain value, the adapter's filter admits what the check denies
in 569 pairs (0.063\% of pairs; 52.5\% of users, 95\% interval 48.3\% to 56.3\%), and the re-check turns
each into a violation (X4-H2 confirmed). Neither translator dropped a negation (X4-H3 confirmed), but OPA's
partial evaluation keeps \texttt{not (x in [])} even for the 73 users with no ban in scope, which falsified
our availability prediction: one ban rule makes that filter unavailable to everyone, who are then
post-filtered.

\paragraph{Revision skew and truncation.}
Unpinned under a writer committing 20 toggles a second, filter and check disagreed in every OpenFGA trial,
in 81.5\% of SpiceDB trials with \texttt{fullyConsistent} (95\% interval 76.0\% to 86.5\%) and in 13.0\% of
OPA trials (8.5\% to 18.0\%). A write between the calls explained every disagreeing pair, and none
reproduced; pinned to one SpiceDB snapshot under the same writer, 200 trials produced no disagreement
(X4-H4 confirmed). An online violation is therefore evidence of a translation fault only when filter and
check read one revision. Non-streaming ListObjects and LookupResources with the layer's limit of 1,000
returned exactly $\min(N, 1000)$ objects without signalling truncation. The layer gave an exact filter for
every $N \le 999$ and none for $N \ge 1000$ in all 540 calls (X4-H5 confirmed), never a short one. The cost
lies at the limit: with 1,000 or 1,001 readable documents among 100,000, the post-filter's 1,000 candidates
hold about ten readable ones, and recall@10 fell to 0.855 to 0.891 per level, against 1.000 with the filter
below the limit and from 3,000 readable documents up (X4-H6 confirmed).

Table~\ref{tab:eng-revoke} gives the measured $\delta$ of each serving path, and a dagger marks a violation
of the $\delta$ the path states. The cache's time to live is the realized delay (every ttl median lies
within 34\,ms of 5\,s or 30\,s; X7-H2 confirmed), and invalidation ends the delay at once, or within the
shared replica's 1\,s local copy. Permissions copied into chunk metadata were revoked within milliseconds
(X7-H4 confirmed). Two sources of truth were stale themselves. Cerbos reloaded a rewritten policy file in a
median 2.5\,s (at most 4.0\,s). SpiceDB under \texttt{minimizeLatency}, the layer's default, took a median
3.3\,s; in 28 polls a revoked chunk reappeared after it had disappeared, and one of 40 trials took 8.7\,s,
beyond the 6.5\,s we had registered (the documented 5.5\,s plus 1\,s for polling; X7-H5 falsified). A rerun's
40 trials took at most 5.2\,s. Invalidation did not survive such an engine: the next call refilled the cache
from the stale snapshot, so 79 of 80 SpiceDB revocations with invalidation exceeded their bound (medians
30.0 and 31.0\,s; X7-H1 and X7-H3 falsified in these cells), and Cerbos's took as long.

We fixed this and four other defects the run exposed. After an invalidation the cache now caches nothing for
the engine's declared staleness (11\,s for SpiceDB without full consistency). A targeted rerun, an
implementation check specified in writing after the main results and before running, confirmed every
prediction (A2-H1 to A2-H8): all 160 SpiceDB and Cerbos revocations with invalidation took effect within the
declared staleness plus 1\,s, and 1\,s more on the replica, at medians of 4.8\,s (at most 5.2\,s) and 3.9\,s
against 30 and 31\,s before. SpiceDB itself still flapped; the cache no longer prolongs that.

We injected 14 faults into each engine, by a proxy or by killing the server, and five or eight into pgvector
and Qdrant, in 1,090 episodes of a 3\,s baseline, a 12\,s fault and recovery. Outside the control below, no
retrieval returned a chunk outside the user's readable set, in 623,279 requests over 1,050 episodes: at most
$2.8 \times 10^{-3}$ per episode (one-sided 95\%), over the faults we injected (X7-H6 confirmed). The control,
40 episodes that turned the engine's denials into allows, leaked in every engine. Every engine-caused failure
was the layer's authorization error with a message naming no host; transport faults failed every request
inside the fault, and recovery took a median 0.02 to 0.18\,s after removal. Two behaviors needed work: an
empty LookupResources stream read as ``no readable documents'' (4,746 retrievals returned nothing without an
error, losing recall only), and store failures surfaced as the clients' own exceptions. The rerun confirmed
both fixes, and in its 10,303 requests over 140 episodes no chunk leaked ($2.1 \times 10^{-2}$ per episode).
For a connection that is accepted but never answered, only a deadline on the whole retrieval, which the layer
does not yet offer, would bound the wait.
