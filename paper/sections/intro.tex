A retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) assistant answers from the chunks of an index that are most
similar to the question~\cite{lewis2020retrievalaugmented}. In an organization those chunks come
from documents with different readers: a payroll file, a board deck, one customer's contract and a
public handbook can share one index. Similarity knows nothing of who may read what, so without
enforcement the chunk most similar to an employee's question may be one that employee may not open,
and the model will repeat it. OWASP lists this as LLM08, vector and embedding
weaknesses~\cite{owasp2025llm082025}; assistants that inherit the permissions of their sources
depend on those permissions being enforced at retrieval time~\cite{microsoft2026data}, and revoked
documents have stayed retrievable through a cached index~\cite{roychowdhury2024confusedpilot}.

The remedy is old. Query modification adds the access predicate to the query before it
runs~\cite{stonebraker1974access}; multi-user full-text search integrated security into query
processing and showed that statistics computed over unreadable documents reveal
them~\cite{buttcher2005security}; enterprise search checked results late, in growing
blocks~\cite{bailey2006secure}; Zanzibar checks each result against a central authorization
service~\cite{pang2019zanzibar}. Managed RAG services combine these steps: Amazon Bedrock knowledge
bases pre-filter by ACL metadata and verify each document against its source before
answering~\cite{aws2026access}. Recent work builds permissions into the vector index
itself~\cite{zhong2026honeybee,han2026dont}, and a 2026 vision paper asks for correctness criteria
for such systems~\cite{yalamarthi2026policyaware}.

What is not known is whether the stack that implements the remedy is faithful. Three things stand
between an organization's policy and the chunks a model sees. \emph{First, the policy is compiled by
translators.} Authorization engines expose filter APIs, such as OpenFGA's ListObjects, SpiceDB's
LookupResources, OPA's partial evaluation and Cerbos's query
plans~\cite{openfga2026perform,authzed2026protecting,opa2026evaluating,cerbos2026cerbos}, which are
code paths separate from their check APIs, and every store has its own filter language with its own
handling of types, missing fields, Unicode and empty lists. We found no published guarantee that the
paths agree. \emph{Second, the filter runs inside an approximate index.} Permission filters are hard
cases for filtered nearest-neighbor search: they are often selective, they take a disjunction over
many groups, and organizational structure, not meaning, decides which chunks they admit, so the
readable set may lie far from the query~\cite{patel2024acorn,lim2026revisiting}. \emph{Third,
failures are silent.} Under Truman semantics~\cite{rizvi2004extending} a person who receives three
chunks, or none, cannot tell whether that is all they may read or all that the index found.

We study permission-aware retrieval as deployed: an enforcement layer that compiles an
organization's existing policy into filters for production vector stores, re-checks every hit with
the authorizer and fails closed. We test one such layer, \sys{}, an open-source library with
adapters for 14 vector stores and seven authorization engines (Section~\ref{sec:system}), and use it
as an instrument. Our starting point is that the re-check rests security on a small trusted computing
base around the authorizer's check API (Observation~\ref{prop:reduction}): a faulty filter or a lossy
index can then cost recall but cannot put a chunk outside the readable set into an answer. The open
questions become how much is lost, where, how fresh the check is, what the answers still reveal, and
whether anyone can tell. Our answer is the title, with its scope stated. By \emph{leak-free} we mean
one thing: in every store and engine we tested, no returned chunk lay outside the subject's readable
set at the revision the re-check read. That revision can be stale: a revoked permission kept admitting
for up to a cache's time to live or an engine's staleness, which we report per path, and the release
we evaluated exceeded its stated bound after an invalidation. Other observables of an answer still revealed
information about unreadable chunks. And retrieval quality under permission-shaped filters ranged from
near perfect to missing most of what a person may read, and the application could not see the loss.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{A formulation} (Section~\ref{sec:problem}). We adapt the sound, secure and maximal
        criteria of Wang et al.~\cite{wang2007correctness} to top-$k$ retrieval under an external
        authorizer, define the readable set from the policy, and add freshness, with a measured
        staleness $\delta$ per path, and count-honesty for the claim that an answer is complete. We
        state the trusted computing base and define metrics: a leak rate with bounds per question and
        per subject, permission-recall@$k$ against exact search over the readable set, and
        under-retrieval and false-completeness rates, reported on measured query-correlation axes.
  \item \emph{Differential conformance testing} (Section~\ref{sec:conformance}). A kit generates
        corpora, permissions and filters, runs them through each store's filter translation and each
        engine's filter and check APIs, and compares every answer with one reference semantics. We ran
        it against 13 of the 14 stores the layer supports (Pinecone, a hosted service, was skipped for
        want of an API key) and seven engines. It found two undocumented store behaviors with a
        correctness effect, Redis and MongoDB truncating long values when indexing and so missing
        matching chunks; two faults in the layer's OpenFGA and SpiceDB adapters, which cost
        availability; and documented size limits in five stores, which fail closed. It also confirmed,
        at scale, a type gap the project's review had already recorded in the layer's Cerbos translator,
        which maps CEL's \texttt{in} and \texttt{==} to a filter that also accepts scalars, so the filter
        over-approximates the policy and the re-check drops the excess. We fixed or mitigated the
        adapter faults and the Redis and MongoDB misses, and a second campaign of 4,480 runs confirmed
        the fixes; in it about 85\% of the OpenFGA and SpiceDB runs were refused by the layer before
        reaching the engine, because the generated subject could not be represented there.
  \item \emph{Filter-versus-check agreement of authorization engines} (Section~\ref{sec:engines}),
        measured at a single revision so that permission changes between calls are not counted as
        faults. Over 6.3 million user-document pairs from OpenFGA, SpiceDB, OPA and Cerbos, in the
        profiles that hold the model as intended, filters and checks never disagreed; the only
        disagreement at one revision was the Cerbos type gap, on one-value ACL fields, and those seen
        in practice were revision skew between the two calls. Four pre-registered verdicts were
        falsified, by three findings: SpiceDB's default consistency served revoked chunks for up to
        8.7\,s, in one of 40 trials (a rerun stayed within 5.2\,s); invalidating a cache in front of a
        stale engine merely refilled it for its full time to live, which we fixed and confirmed in the
        rerun; and a single ban rule removed OPA's pre-filter for everyone. No chunk outside the
        readable set was returned in 1,050 fault episodes, a one-sided 95\% bound of
        $2.8 \times 10^{-3}$ per episode over the faults we injected.
  \item \emph{An audit of retrieval quality under permission filters} (Section~\ref{sec:quality}).
        In an enterprise scenario of 107,540 chunks and 20 tenants, mean recall@10 at common settings
        over four runs ranged from 0.55 to 0.98 over eight stores searched approximately, under this
        layer's schema. Pre-registered experiments trace Qdrant's loss to an islanded permitted
        subgraph (recall@10 of 0.656 in the shared layout and 0.952 with per-tenant graphs) and show
        that pgvector's answer depends on a decision taken before any search: PostgreSQL's plan cache
        chooses an exact plan or the HNSW scan according to the state of a GIN index's pending list.
        Over eight runs the same configuration ran exact in five and, by its median, fast in three, one
        of them a mixture, and in the fast runs it lost about a quarter of its recall, a split consistent
        with autovacuum running on its own schedule. When it scans the graph, both of the scan's limits
        cut answers short. On a million MS MARCO passages under controlled permission layouts, recall@10
        at the stores' defaults ranged from 0.44 to above 0.99. On clustered layouts with negatively
        correlated questions, filters admitting 0.5\% to 10\% of the corpus were harder than the smallest
        (0.06\%) in five of nine stores, and such questions cost four stores 0.05 to 0.42 of recall and
        others latency instead.
  \item \emph{Detection and bounded repair of under-retrieval} (Section~\ref{sec:repair}): a
        completeness flag that never certifies a short approximate answer, count-based detection,
        and an exact fallback with a cost bound. The count flagged all 2,657 truly short answers
        without a false alarm, and exact repair without a bound left none empty or short, at a median of
        30 to 109\,ms on the answers it repaired. A bound limits this: on MS MARCO, a bound of 1,000 chunks
        repaired only 0.5\% of pgvector's answers out of the 1.7\% that triggered it, and bounds of 1,000
        and 10,000 repaired none of Qdrant's, whose short answers all had more readable chunks. No repair
        touches mis-retrieval, and 57\% to 58\% of Qdrant's enterprise answers marked complete were not
        maximal under every policy, against 24\% with per-tenant graphs.
  \item \emph{Side channels of filtered retrieval} (Section~\ref{sec:side}): what latency, counts,
        recall and scores reveal about unreadable chunks near a query. In a pre-registered
        distinguishing game, a subject who repeated a query could tell that 100 or more unreadable
        chunks had appeared near it from the latency of the widening post-filter (paired AUC 0.80
        to 0.98, except pgvector's 0.66 for an attacker reading 10\% of the background) and of
        pgvector's approximate pre-filter (1.00 with 1,000 of them), and from BM25 and IDF scores (0.98
        to 1.00). Two FAISS builds of identical content themselves gave paired AUCs of 0.36 to 0.60, so
        weaker channels lie near build noise. Exact pre-filtering returned identical answers, and padding, tested
        where the unpadded AUC reached 0.75, removed the latency channel in all seven such cells, at five
        to seven times the latency.
  \item \emph{An end-to-end evaluation with canaries} (Section~\ref{sec:e2e}): four runs of 1,105
        questions per configuration, 432 of them red-team prompts, over 189 people and eleven
        configurations of nine stores and three ways to state the policy (in process, OPA and
        OpenFGA), with no leak: a one-sided 95\% bound of $6.8 \times 10^{-4}$ leaks per question for
        each configuration and $1.6 \times 10^{-2}$ per person. Both bounds hold over the generator's
        question distribution and say nothing about an adaptive adversary.
\end{itemize}

We do not propose an index: HoneyBee and Veda are physical designs whose recall our harness can
measure, and the enforcement mechanism has the precedents listed above. Nor do we defend against
prompt injection in readable content; in our runs a 3-billion-parameter model obeyed every injected
notice it retrieved, which marks where a retrieval guarantee ends. The library, the conformance kit,
the pre-registrations and the raw outputs are released with the paper (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).
