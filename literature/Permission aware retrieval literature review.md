# Prior Art Moves wardowl's Novelty Into Verification

The paper should not present wardowl's enforcement design as new. Pushing an authorization predicate into the query is Stonebraker and Wong's 1974 query modification and Büttcher and Clarke's 2005 "query integration". The widening post-filter is the result-checking loop that Bailey, Hawking and Matson published in 2006. Checking every candidate is how Google's Zanzibar serves search, and Amazon Bedrock's managed knowledge bases already ship pre-filtering plus a real-time re-check of each result that fails closed. Three 2026 database papers (HoneyBee, Veda and a SIGMOD-workshop vision paper) also already cover permission-aware vector search. What no reviewed source contains is the part wardowl adds. That is differential conformance testing of filter semantics across many vector stores, and a measured count of disagreements between an authorizer's filter compiler and its own check API, a translation step that has no peer-reviewed semantics. It is also an audit of production stores under realistic permission filters, in which the authors' Kaggle run found recall@10 ranging from 0.56 to 1.00 and empty results for least-privileged pgvector users that the `complete` flag did not catch. The last piece is canary-based enforcement testing at the scale of 163,236 checked hits with zero leaks. Framed as a verification and evaluation paper ("leak-free but not loss-free"), the work fits the NeurIPS Evaluations & Datasets (E&D) track best. Its 2027 deadline is unannounced but likely falls in early May on the 2026 pattern. The fallbacks are PVLDB's Experiment, Analysis & Benchmark (EA&B) category, open monthly until 1 March 2027, and USENIX Security '27 Cycle 2 on 26 January 2027, and NeurIPS's ban on concurrent archival submission means the authors must pick one stream. To reach NeurIPS-level rigor the paper still needs repeated runs with confidence intervals, tuned recall-throughput frontiers instead of default settings, and a measured rather than a named correlation axis. It also needs filter-size, side-channel and revocation experiments, and a bug table confirmed by the store maintainers. Four process risks could sink the submission before anyone reviews it: overlap with prior work, de-anonymization through a public library published under the author's name, AI-assistance disclosure under arXiv's 2026 one-year ban for hallucinated references, and arXiv's new requirement that first-time submitters obtain a personal endorsement.

## Prior work covers the enforcement design but not its verification

Five bodies of work bear on permission-aware retrieval, and they rarely cite each other: enterprise search, database access control, authorization systems, filtered approximate-nearest-neighbour (ANN) search, and RAG security. Each answers part of wardowl's question and leaves a specific gap. The paper should stand in those gaps and credit everything else.

### Enterprise search and databases settled early versus late binding by 2006

The founding result is Büttcher and Clarke's FAST 2005 security model for multi-user file-system search ([Büttcher & Clarke 2005](https://www.usenix.org/legacy/events/fast05/tech/full_papers/buettcher/buettcher.pdf)). Their insecure baseline, the "postprocessing approach", uses one system-wide index ranked with global BM25 statistics and removes unreadable files afterwards. It lets any user who can create files infer how many unreadable files contain a term. With ranks alone, that user can **bound the count to about 0.5% with roughly ten binary-search queries**, and with phrase queries can reconstruct an unreadable file word by word. Their fix intersects every posting list with the user's searchable set before ranking, so statistics never touch hidden content, at a cost of **between 39% faster and 17% slower** than postprocessing; it is fastest when users see a small share of the corpus. Their correctness definition is stricter than "no unreadable file appears": no aspect of the output, including its order, may depend on content the user cannot search.

A year later Bailey, Hawking and Matson turned late binding into a cost model ([Bailey et al. 2006](https://david-hawking.net/pubs/cikm127_bailey.pdf)). Acceptable latency must cover the number of results checked times the per-check latency, divided by visibility density, plus fixed overheads. An off-server LDAP check of **6.4 ms limits a one-second budget to 93 checked results**. Their reference algorithm fetches blocks of NR×2/VD candidates until a page fills, which is wardowl's widening post-filter under another name. They recommended exact counts only below a threshold, checks batched in groups of 100 to 1,000 IDs, and sizing for the least-privileged tenth of users. Google's Search Appliance team supplied the "early binding" and "late binding" vocabulary and the classic objection to early binding: copied ACLs drift out of sync with the source and leak ([Google 2006](https://cloud.googleblog.com/2006/12/document-level-security-with-enterprise.html)).

The database lineage is older and more formal. Stonebraker and Wong rewrote each query by conjoining the qualifications of the applicable access rules, which is exactly what a pushed-down permission filter does ([Byun's summary of Stonebraker & Wong](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/ninghui/courses/Spring05/lectures/QueryModification.pdf)). Rizvi et al. separated two semantics ([Rizvi et al. 2004](https://research.ibm.com/publications/extending-query-rewriting-techniques-for-fine-grained-access-control)). Under "Truman" semantics the system silently answers over the user's restricted view, as Oracle VPD does. Under "non-Truman" semantics it rejects queries that cannot be answered from authorized data.

Wang et al. then set three correctness criteria and found no algorithm of the time that satisfied the first two together ([Wang et al. 2007](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/ninghui/papers/fgdb_vldb07.pdf)). An answer is *sound* if it is consistent with the unrestricted answer, *secure* if it leaks nothing the policy forbids, and *maximal* if it returns as much as the first two allow. Kabra et al. showed that functions evaluated on rows before the authorization predicate leak through their values and their error messages ([Kabra et al. 2006](https://repository.ias.ac.in/128497)). Guarnieri and Basin proved that no optimal security-aware query processing exists for full relational calculus ([Guarnieri & Basin 2014](https://doi.org/10.14778/2732977.2733003)). PostgreSQL row-level security puts these lessons into production with default deny, "leakproof" functions and documented covert channels through unique and foreign-key checks ([PostgreSQL docs](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/ddl-rowsecurity.html)).

So wardowl's pre-filter is query modification with Truman semantics, its post-filter is Bailey's loop, and its re-check deliberately keeps the redundancy that Kabra et al. tried to optimize away. Büttcher and Clarke's statistics attack does not transfer directly to pure dense retrieval, because the cosine score of one query-document pair ignores collection statistics. It does transfer to hybrid BM25-plus-vector scoring, IDF-weighted sparse embeddings, and any score normalization or rank fusion computed over a candidate set that includes unauthorized items. The paper's threat model must say which of these wardowl's stores use.

### Authorization engines emit filters through translators nobody has verified

Zanzibar stores more than two trillion ACL tuples and answers millions of checks a second at p95 under 10 ms, yet it has **no "list everything this user can read" API** ([Pang et al. 2019](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/atc19-pang.pdf)). Google's search is late-bound, with Drive issuing tens to hundreds of checks per result page; clients that want early binding build their own indexes from Expand and Watch. Snapshot tokens ("zookies") prevent the "new enemy" problem of applying an old ACL to new content, and the paper notes that without an explicit authorization, client services would be forced to deny access.

The open-source descendants added reverse lookups but advise caution in using them. OpenFGA recommends ListObjects only for about 1,000 accessible objects, and its server stops a non-streaming call at **1,000 results or 3 seconds by default** ([OpenFGA search guide](https://openfga.dev/docs/interacting/search-with-permissions); [OpenFGA configuration](https://openfga.dev/docs/getting-started/setup-openfga/configuration)). SpiceDB recommends LookupResources below about 10,000 resources and a bulk-check loop pinned to one revision token otherwise ([AuthZed list endpoints](https://authzed.com/docs/spicedb/modeling/protecting-a-list-endpoint)). Its default `minimize_latency` reads leave a window for the new-enemy problem ([AuthZed consistency](https://authzed.com/docs/spicedb/concepts/consistency)). A truncated allow-list is safe but loses recall without telling anyone.

Policy engines compile policies into filters through engineering artefacts, not published formalisms. OPA's partial evaluation (since v0.9, 2018) turns residual conditions into SQL WHERE clauses, but only for a documented fragment of Rego ([OPA blog](https://openpolicyagent.org/blog/write-policy-in-opa-enforce-policy-in-sql-d9d24db93bf4); [OPA fragment](https://openpolicyagent.org/docs/filtering/fragment)). Cerbos's PlanResources returns always-allowed, always-denied or a conditional syntax tree and ships a LangChain/ChromaDB adapter ([Cerbos API](https://docs.cerbos.dev/cerbos/latest/api/index.html)). The peer-reviewed Cedar paper does not discuss filtering ([Cutler et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.04651)), and XACML 3.0 returns one decision per request ([XACML 3.0](http://docs.oasis-open.org/xacml/3.0/xacml-3.0-core-spec-os-en.html)). The review found **no peer-reviewed semantics or correctness proof for any of these policy-to-filter translators**.

That gap is the strongest justification for wardowl's re-check, and it points to the paper's cleanest formal statement. Because every returned hit must pass the authorizer's check API, security depends on the check API alone, and errors in the filter translator can only cost recall. The reduction has a blind side the paper must state. The re-check sees false admits, which are counted as violations, but not false denials, where an over-restrictive filter silently drops authorized documents. Only differential conformance testing or an exact-search audit can find those. Zanzibar's consistency analysis adds a requirement: filter and re-check must be evaluated at one revision. Otherwise a counted "violation" may be a legitimate ACL change between the two calls rather than a translation bug.

### Filtered vector search explains the recall collapse, not permission-shaped filters

The ANN literature splits filtered search into pre-filtering, post-filtering and in-traversal filtering, and explains the post-filter collapse precisely. With pass fraction s, a post-filter needs about K/s candidates when permitted items are spread evenly, and O(n) in the worst case when they lie far from the query ([ACORN](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.04871v1)). Independent groups have measured the collapse. ACORN's post-filter could not exceed 0.9 recall on LAION-1M even with positively correlated filters ([ACORN](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.04871v1)). Faiss-HNSW and ACORN "fail completely" at 0.1% selectivity, where only partitioned Milvus and Faiss-IVFPQ cope ([Li et al., SIGMOD 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.16263v2)). Without a fallback, ACORN-1's recall falls to **0.45–0.72 at 1% and 0.03–0.10 at 0.3%** pass fraction ([RACORN-1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.00768)). pgvector's README notes that a filter matching 10% of rows with the default ef_search of 40 yields about four matches ([pgvector](https://github.com/pgvector/pgvector)). Every major engine now falls back to exact scoring for restrictive filters. OpenSearch, since version 3.5, runs exact search whenever approximate results number fewer than k while at least k documents pass ([Qdrant](https://qdrant.tech/articles/vector-search-filtering/); [Weaviate](https://weaviate.io/blog/speed-up-filtered-vector-search); [OpenSearch](https://docs.opensearch.org/latest/vector-search/filter-search-knn/efficient-knn-filtering/)).

Nothing in this literature studies the shape of real permission filters. Filtered-DiskANN cites enterprise users' privileges as motivation. It still evaluates only single-label filters and suggests handling OR by searching each label separately and merging the results ([Filtered-DiskANN](https://harsha-simhadri.org/pubs/Filtered-DiskANN23.pdf)). The best-known benchmark, the NeurIPS'23 Big-ANN filtered track, uses AND filters of one or two tags from a vocabulary of 200,386 and does not analyse correlation ([Big-ANN results](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.17424v1)). Newer benchmarks control correlation explicitly through VecBench ([VecBench](https://doi.org/10.1145/3802125)), the Global-Local Selectivity metric ([Amanbayev et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11443)) and HCBGen's α-Hardness. HCBGen also shows that selectivity and correlation alone are unstable proxies for difficulty ([HCBGen](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.14193)). No paper found evaluates a per-query OR over hundreds or thousands of group labels, which is the shape of a directory-group ACL, and no benchmark uses real ACLs.

Permission-specific vector work is recent and assumes role-based access control (RBAC). HoneyBee (SIGMOD 2026) partitions by role structure and reports **up to 13.5× lower latency than PostgreSQL row-level security at 1.24× memory**. Its permissions are synthetic (1,000 users, 100 roles), and its cost model explicitly ignores query-permission correlation ([HoneyBee](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.01538); [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.01538v3)). Veda builds an access-aware lattice over 641 to 757 role combinations ([Veda](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.01342v3)). The SeQureDB '26 policy-aware vision paper finds that on pgvector pre-filtering gives the best recall, naive post-filtering gives "near-zero recall" for selective policies, and iterative scans raise latency sharply. It explicitly asks for Wang et al.'s sound, secure and maximal criteria to be re-interpreted for vector search ([Yalamarthi & Pappachan 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.19803v1)). Curator addresses multi-tenancy without a security model ([Curator](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.07119)). An October 2026 preprint attributes per-tenant recall loss partly to the local density of points that satisfy a tenant's predicate ([Das et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.15130)).

Two consequences follow. The "aligned" and "misaligned" layouts are positive and negative query-filter correlation, an axis the field already has, so the paper must measure where each layout sits on it rather than present it as new. And the regimes the paper can own are OR filters at directory-group scale, relationship-based permissions and ACL churn.

### RAG security demands deterministic enforcement and leaves retrieval side channels open

Since 2024, RAG security work has shown that anyone who can query a RAG system can drain its store. Zeng et al. extracted private retrieval data with composite prompts. In their tests re-ranking had "almost no mitigation effects" and summarization cut untargeted risk by only about half ([Zeng et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.16893)). Qi et al. leaked the datastores of **25 of 25 custom GPTs with at most two queries each** ([Qi et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.17840)), and CopyBreakRAG extracted over 70% of protected knowledge bases ([Jiang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.14110)). Interrogation attacks infer membership with about 30 natural-looking queries ([Naseh et al., CCS 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.00306)). A 2026 survey of 118 RAG defence papers found that an 8-gram output guard cut verbatim copying to 0.0%, yet an adaptive three-query attacker still recovered **48.3% of hidden facts** ([Liu et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30193)). The defences these papers recommend are probabilistic and sit on the model side.

The work on cross-tenant leakage arrives at wardowl's premise. Arceo and Narsing observe that retrieval ranks "by relevance ... not by authorization", and report that attribute-based gating at retrieval eliminated cross-tenant leakage ([Arceo & Narsing, CAIS '26](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.05287)). In hybrid vector-plus-graph RAG, retrieval-pivot attacks reach a risk score of 0.95, and re-checking authorization at the graph-expansion boundary drives leakage close to zero ([Thornton 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08668)). That result implies wardowl must also re-check parent documents, neighbouring chunks and graph hops added after the vector stage. Microsoft researchers call prompt sanitization and output filtering "fundamentally probabilistic". They also require content to be authorized for every participant in an interaction, a multi-user case beyond wardowl's single-subject model ([Bhatt et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.14608)). The OrgAccess benchmark shows why enforcement cannot be left to prompts: GPT-4.1 reaches only **F1 = 0.27** on its hardest set ([OrgAccess](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.19165)). OWASP's LLM08:2025 makes "permission-aware vector and embedding stores" its first prevention measure ([OWASP](https://genai.owasp.org/llmrisk/llm082025-vector-and-embedding-weaknesses/)). PermLLM, a parametric alternative, appeared in the NeurIPS 2025 main track, which shows that access control for LLMs is in scope there ([PermLLM](https://papers.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/754612bde73a8b65ad8743f1f6d8ddf6-Abstract-Conference.html)).

Two neighbouring literatures bound wardowl's claims. The first is embedding inversion. vec2text recovers **92% of 32-token inputs exactly** ([Morris et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06816)), and zero-shot inversion no longer needs the target encoder ([ZSInvert](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.00147)). The guarantee therefore covers the query path only: the vector store still needs storage-level protection, and wardowl must never return or log raw vectors, query embeddings included.

The second is agent identity, the classic confused-deputy problem ([Hardy 1988](https://doi.org/10.1145/54289.871709)), where standards have converged on wardowl's answer. RFC 8693's `act` claim names the delegated actor while the top-level subject's rights govern access ([RFC 8693](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8693.txt)). The 2026-07-28 MCP specification requires audience-bound tokens and forbids passing a client's token through to upstream APIs ([MCP authorization](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2026-07-28/basic/authorization)). wardowl's calls to an upstream authorizer therefore need their own credential. Because MCP's STDIO transport takes credentials from the environment, a verified identity per request is available only over HTTP. MITRE ATLAS mitigation AML.M0027 says an agent should never hold permissions its user would not ([ATLAS 2026.09](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mitre-atlas/atlas-data/main/dist/v6/ATLAS-2026.09.yaml)). The finding that **40.55% of 7,973 live remote MCP servers expose tools without authentication** is concrete motivation for verifying tokens by default ([Zhou et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.22333)). Huang et al.'s study of caller-identity confusion in MCP was withdrawn by its authors and must not be cited ([arXiv 2603.07473](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07473)).

Real incidents fall into four classes, and wardowl addresses the first two. The first is enforcement bugs. Azure AI Search preview APIs returned all ACL-protected documents to callers who used an API key and sent no user token, until November 2025 ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/azure/search/search-query-access-control-rbac-enforcement)). A logic flaw in Asana's MCP server exposed data across organizations, reportedly affecting about 1,000 customers ([The Register](https://theregister.com/2025/06/18/asana_mcp_server_bug/)). The second is stale state: in ConfusedPilot's Attack 5, revoked documents stayed retrievable through Copilot's cache ([ConfusedPilot](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.04870)). The third is faithful enforcement of over-broad ACLs; one vendor reports about three million sensitive records accessible to Copilot per organization ([Help Net Security](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2025/09/25/generative-ai-data-risk-exposure/)). The fourth is confused-deputy exfiltration after correct retrieval, of which EchoLeak (CVSS 9.3) is the type case ([NVD](https://nvd.nist.gov/vuln/detail/CVE-2025-32711)). The authors' own run reproduces class four: Qwen2.5-3B obeyed every retrieved injected notice, for example 106 of 106 on LanceDB ([Kaggle run](../evidence/x0-scale-run.md)). The paper should use EchoLeak to mark the limit of its claim, not to motivate it.

One question is untouched across all five bodies: side channels in filtered vector search. Under Büttcher and Clarke's consistency definition, wardowl's widening loop is a candidate channel. Its number of rounds, its latency and its `complete` flag all depend on how many unauthorized vectors lie near the query, and the same holds for graph traversal that passes through unauthorized nodes. Timing channels in RAG infrastructure are already practical: SpliceLeak recovers hidden prompt content in as few as 63 requests per token ([SpliceLeak](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.21842)). The review found no measurement of this channel in filtered vector search, so the paper can either measure it or must at least state it.

### Industry ships pre-filter plus re-check, one store or engine at a time

Among native store features, only Azure AI Search (preview) and Elasticsearch/OpenSearch document-level security enforce on the server, per user. Azure goes furthest. It builds filters from the caller's Entra token, expands groups live through Microsoft Graph, and returns a 5xx error rather than "a partially filtered result set" when evaluation fails. Even so, its document ACLs are only as fresh as the last sync ([Azure query-time enforcement](https://learn.microsoft.com/azure/search/search-query-access-control-rbac-enforcement)). Qdrant, Weaviate, Pinecone and Milvus authorize at collection, tenant or namespace granularity. Qdrant removed payload filters from JWT RBAC in 1.16, citing inconsistent read and write semantics ([Qdrant v1.16.0](https://github.com/qdrant/qdrant/releases/tag/v1.16.0)).

Authorization vendors each ship an integration for one engine. Auth0's FGARetriever is a post-filter; Cerbos query plans and Oso's SQL filters are pre-filters; langchain-spicedb offers both ([Auth0](https://auth0.com/ai/docs/intro/authorization-for-rag); [langchain-spicedb](https://github.com/authzed/langchain-spicedb); [Cerbos recipe](https://docs.cerbos.dev/cerbos/latest/recipes/ai/rag-authorization/)). None of the reviewed artefacts widens until k authorized hits arrive, and none re-checks pre-filtered hits. OpenFGA's whole answer to truncation is that post-filtering "may return fewer than K", plus a tip to over-fetch two to three times ([OpenFGA RAG guide](https://openfga.dev/docs/modeling/agents/rag-authorization.md)).

Enterprise assistants mirror ACLs into their indexes, so revocation waits for the next connector sync; Coveo's Dynamics provider, for instance, refreshes group mappings daily ([Coveo](https://docs.coveo.com/en/448/)). The closest commercial analogue to wardowl is **Amazon Bedrock's managed knowledge-base ACL awareness** ([AWS](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/bedrock/latest/userguide/kb-managed-acl.md)). It pre-filters on crawled ACLs, then makes "a real-time call to the data source to verify that the user still has access to each returned document". Deny overrides allow, and AWS states that "a transient failure never results in documents being returned to unauthorized users" while also calling the feature "not a security boundary".

No project found combines many vector stores with many external permission engines. langchain-spicedb works with any store but only one engine, and Pebblo supports several stores with metadata only ([Pebblo](https://python.langchain.com/docs/integrations/providers/pebblo/pebblo_retrieval_qa)). A new pgvector-only repository reports zero leaks in 330 adversarial tests on a 60-document corpus ([Clearance](https://github.com/gelevanog/permission-aware-rag)). wardowl's breadth is therefore distinctive, but it is an engineering claim and should be phrased as "none found".

### Testing practice stops at relational engines and synthetic permissions

Logic-bug testing has mature oracles for SQL and graph databases. Pivoted Query Synthesis found 121 bugs in SQLite, MySQL and PostgreSQL ([Rigger & Su, OSDI 2020](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi20/presentation/rigger)). NoREC and Ternary Logic Partitioning turned bug-finding into comparing a query with a non-optimizing or partitioned rewrite of itself ([NoREC](https://doi.org/10.1145/3368089.3409710); [TLP](https://doi.org/10.1145/3428279)), and the partitioning oracle has since moved to graph databases ([Kamm et al.](https://doi.org/10.1145/3597926.3598044)). Vector databases have only a bug study and a roadmap. The study of 1,671 bug-fix pull requests across 15 vector databases found that **57.3% of bugs were functional failures** and named "filter abnormality" as a fault class. It never mentions differential or metamorphic testing ([Xie et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.02617v1)). The 2030 testing roadmap lists oracle definition as an open challenge ([Wang et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.20812)). wardowl's canary suite descends from the Secret Sharer's inserted canaries ([Carlini et al. 2019](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity19/presentation/carlini)), turned from a memorization estimate into a deterministic enforcement check.

No public corpus pairs documents with real read ACLs. The TREC Enterprise collections were crawled from public-facing sites ([TREC 2008 Enterprise](https://pages.nist.gov/trec-browser/trec17/enterprise/overview)). The public access-control data, such as the HP Labs role-mining matrices with densities from 0.003 to 0.702 ([Molloy et al. 2009](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/ninghui/papers/eval_role_mining_sacmat09.pdf)) and Amazon's employee access logs ([Nobi et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.01739)), contains no document text. Prior work therefore synthesizes permissions. HoneyBee reuses Molloy's random, tree and ERBAC generators. PermLLM (also NeurIPS 2025) builds security domains from RCV1 topics and from PubMedQA clusters ([PermLLM PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.22860)), and EnronQA's 150 real inboxes offer a semi-real alternative ([EnronQA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.00263)). On reporting, the ANN community plots recall-throughput Pareto frontiers ([ANN-Benchmarks](https://github.com/erikbern/ann-benchmarks)) and ranks systems by throughput at a fixed recall. Yet even the NeurIPS 2025 Big-ANN paper and HoneyBee report **no error bars** ([Big-ANN](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.17424v1); [HoneyBee](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.01538v3)). The ML-statistics literature ([Bouthillier et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.03098); [Agarwal et al.](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2021/file/f514cec81cb148559cf475e7426eed5e-Paper.pdf)) and NeurIPS checklist item 7 ([NeurIPS checklist](https://neurips.cc/public/guides/PaperChecklist)) set a bar that wardowl can clear cheaply and so exceed its own sub-field.

## A novelty ledger shows the contribution is verification, not filtering

The ledger compares each element of the draft plan's contributions ([paper plan v0](../plan/paper-plan-v0.md)) with its closest prior work. "Not novel" does not mean "drop it". It means describe the element as a design choice and cite its lineage.

The authors' evidence supports the novel rows and also exposes a weakness. The Kaggle run of wardowl 2.1.0 used 107,540 chunks, 20 tenants, 189 people and 1,105 questions, 432 of them red-team prompts, across ten store configurations. It checked **163,236 hits with zero unreadable hits, zero canary leaks and zero violations** ([Kaggle run](../evidence/x0-scale-run.md)). The same run produced the paper's most interesting finding. At the settings used, recall@10 ran from **0.561 for Qdrant** through 0.732 for pgvector to 1.000 for exact FAISS. pgvector returned nothing to ten least-privileged users who could read documents, and the `complete` flag stayed true throughout.

The authors' follow-up investigation traces both failures to the scenario's misalignment ([recall-gap investigation](../investigations/filtered-recall-gaps.md)). A typical user may read about 54% of the corpus but only 2–5% of the chunks near a company-document question. Qdrant's filtered traversal discards neighbours that fail the filter, builds no extra payload edges for values as common as the tenant field, and ran without ACORN. Qdrant's own benchmark shows ACORN lifting recall from 39.7% to 100% at a 4% filter intersection. pgvector's iterative scan stops after 20,000 visited elements, or 4 MB of scan memory at the default `work_mem`. wardowl marks an empty pre-filtered result complete unconditionally. About 96% of pgvector's recall loss is mis-retrieval, meaning k hits came back but not the right ones, rather than empty results. Some of these causal claims are still marked unverified in that file.

These diagnoses make the finding more convincing, but they change how it must be presented. A table at default settings alone invites the "unfair baseline" criticism that ACORN's authors themselves raised against prior work ([ACORN](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.04871v1)). Detecting under-retrieval is not new either: OpenSearch 3.5 triggers its exact fallback when a count shows at least k readable documents but fewer than k came back. The robust claim is narrower: **at their documented settings, today's stores silently lose recall under permission-shaped filters, through store-specific mechanisms that a library cannot see without an exact-search audit or a count-certified completeness check.**

The formal frame should come from the database lineage, because the vision paper explicitly asks for it. Soundness is returning the true top-k of the authorized sub-corpus. Security is guaranteed by the re-check whatever the filter translator does, and `violations` measures how insecure the pushed-down filter is. `complete` certifies maximality only once it is backed by an exact search or a count, which wardowl 2.1.0 does not yet do. The semantics are Truman semantics, with `complete` as a partial step toward the non-Truman model. On this frame the plan's third working title, "Leak-Free but Not Loss-Free", is accurate. The paper must not claim to be the first permission-aware vector search, since HoneyBee, Veda and the vision paper came earlier. Nor should it claim to have discovered post-filter collapse at low selectivity, to have introduced alignment as an evaluation axis, or that fail-closed behaviour is a contribution in itself.

## Closing the rigor gap takes repeated runs, tuned frontiers and four new experiments

As the plan stands, the existing benchmark is a single run at default index settings on one machine, with no confidence intervals (evaluation notes, kept outside this repository). Its headline numbers are consistent with ACORN's worst case: in-index pre-filtering recall of 0.95–1.00 against **0.000 for naive top-k-then-filter** at 0.9% readable in the misaligned layout (filtered-search notes, kept outside this repository). Reviewers will still read one run as an anecdote. The statistics should follow the protocol already drafted in the authors' investigation. Comparisons should be paired on the same questions, with 95% confidence intervals from a cluster bootstrap over persons (10,000 resamples). McNemar's test fits the empty-result rate and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test fits per-question recall, and effects should be reported with their intervals rather than as bare p-values. On top of that, the paper needs at least five layout seeds and three index builds for each build-time configuration, since query-time settings are deterministic on a fixed build. Latency should be the median of three passes after warm-up, reported at p50, p95 and p99, with thread count, batch mode and cache state stated. Following checklist item 7, the paper should say whether each error bar is a standard deviation or a confidence interval.

Zero-leak claims need an honest upper bound. By the rule of three, zero events in n independent trials bound the event rate below about 3/n at 95% confidence (this is standard arithmetic, not a figure from the sources). Hits cluster by question and person, so they are not independent trials. The defensible bound is **about 2.7×10⁻³ per question across 1,105 questions** and about 1.6% per person across 189 people, not 1.8×10⁻⁵ per hit. Showing the bound for more than one unit pre-empts the obvious reviewer question.

Checklist item 8 asks for compute. The run used 2× Tesla T4, 4 CPUs, 31.3 GiB RAM and 2.8 hours, and a second machine type would answer the single-machine objection. If generated layouts or the canary scenario are released as data, E&D requires a Croissant file with fields such as `rai:hasSyntheticData` and `prov:wasDerivedFrom` ([E&D hosting](https://neurips.cc/Conferences/2026/EvaluationsDatasetsHosting)). Nobody has yet checked the FiQA and MS MARCO licences for derived data.

## Anonymity, AI disclosure, endorsement and bug disclosure can sink the paper before review

Anonymity costs more than usual here because the artifact itself identifies the author. E&D review is double-blind by default; single-blind is allowed only when a dataset cannot be anonymized, and code must be anonymized and executable at submission ([E&D FAQ](https://neurips.cc/Conferences/2026/EvaluationsDatasetsFAQ)). wardowl is public on PyPI and GitHub under the author's name. The submitted copy therefore needs, in this report's judgement rather than by any venue rule, a neutral system name and a renamed package in an anonymized mirror; the FAQ allows services such as anonymous.4open.science. Links to issues the author filed upstream must also be anonymized, and the Croissant file must carry no names. Non-anonymous arXiv preprints are permitted at NeurIPS, ICML, USENIX and S&P, but none may be advertised as under submission. NeurIPS warns that aggressive promotion can be a violation, and USENIX asks authors not to time preprints to signal identity ([NeurIPS handbook](https://neurips.cc/Conferences/2026/MainTrackHandbook); [USENIX CFP](https://www.usenix.org/node/321195)). Library release notes and posts made during review should not mention the paper.

AI-assistance disclosure varies by venue, so the strictest rule should set the text. Every venue bars AI authorship. NeurIPS requires disclosure only when AI is an important or non-standard part of the method and exempts "basic code assistance". ICLR 2027 mandates an AI-use section and counts using AI to "implement methods" as a required disclosure ([ICLR AI policy](https://iclr.cc/Conferences/2027/AIPolicyForAuthors)). S&P 2027 requires a statement even for editorial use. arXiv requires authors to report significant use of text-generating AI ([arXiv moderation](https://info.arxiv.org/help/moderation/index.html)). A coding agent that wrote substantial parts of the library, the conformance kit or the leak harness goes beyond basic assistance, and so does AI-assisted literature synthesis such as the notes behind this report. Keeping AI tools off the author list and out of commit attribution satisfies the authorship rules but not the disclosure rules.

The sharpest form of the disclosure risk is a bad citation. In about May 2026, arXiv's CS chair announced a **one-year ban for incontrovertible evidence of unchecked LLM output such as hallucinated references**. The announcement was made on X and reported by the press; no arXiv policy page carries it ([Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-01595-5)). NeurIPS treats hallucinated citations as a Code of Conduct violation. The research notes behind this report flag several items to re-check before citing. They include Qi et al.'s claimed ICLR 2025 venue, RAG-Thief's original title, and whether the RecSys 2025 vec2text reproducibility study weakens the 92% figure. The Concentric and ShareGate oversharing figures come from secondary reporting, the MLSys 2027 and ICML 2027 dates from third-party listings, and the PVLDB EA&B rules were read only in search snippets.

arXiv's endorsement and category rules changed in 2026. Since 21 January 2026 an institutional email address alone no longer qualifies. A first-time submitter needs either prior accepted authorship in the section or a personal endorsement from an established author, and mass-emailing potential endorsers is explicitly inappropriate ([arXiv endorsement](https://info.arxiv.org/help/endorsement.html); [arXiv blog](https://blog.arxiv.org/2025/12/10/updated-endorsement-policy-for-arxiv-mathematics/)). Endorsement is per category, so the primary category should be fixed first. cs.CR fits a paper whose core claim is enforcement, cross-listed to cs.IR, cs.DB and cs.LG; the ML classification guide says cs.LG should not be the primary category for applied work ([arXiv guide](https://blog.arxiv.org/2019/12/05/arxiv-machine-learning-classification-guide/)). The licence of a version cannot be changed later, and CC BY 4.0 is the most broadly compatible choice ([arXiv licences](https://info.arxiv.org/help/license/index.html)). The preprint must not be framed as "Position:" or as a survey, because since October 2025 arXiv CS accepts those only after peer-reviewed acceptance ([arXiv blog](https://blog.arxiv.org/2025/10/31/attention-authors-updated-practice-for-review-articles-and-position-papers-in-arxiv-cs-category/)). arXiv also updated its submission rate limits on 1 October 2026 ([arXiv blog](https://blog.arxiv.org/2026/10/01/updated-rate-limit-policy/)).

Finally, vulnerability disclosure governs when the preprint can appear. The conformance kit found real defects in third-party stores. S&P requires disclosure by the rebuttal deadline and an ethics statement, and USENIX may reject papers on ethical grounds ([S&P CFP](https://sp2027.ieee-security.org/cfpapers.html)). A public arXiv preprint describing undisclosed bugs would breach both venues' norms. Vendors must be notified before the preprint, which is why the ML-stream plan places arXiv after disclosure has begun rather than now.

## Conclusion

The literature changes what wardowl's paper is about. Enforcing permissions inside retrieval is a fifty-year-old idea with industrial implementations. What nobody verifies is the next step. A permission filter is compiled by one system and executed by another, and no one checks that it means what the authorizer means, or that the store's approximate search returns everything the filter admits. The authors' own data shows the safety side holding, with zero leaks over 163,236 checked hits, and the recall side failing silently in store-specific ways. That asymmetry is the finding. A design that reduces security to the authorizer's check API turns every remaining error into lost recall, and lost recall stays invisible unless something counts it. The paper's lasting contribution is therefore the instrument, not the library: conformance oracles, violation counting, count-certified completeness and canary harnesses that anyone can point at any store.

Two strategic points follow. The most original research question within reach is the one this review found unstudied everywhere: whether widening loops, completeness flags and filtered graph traversal leak information about the documents they exclude. One careful experiment on that question would strengthen the novelty case more than any further store integration. And the field is moving fast. HoneyBee, Veda, the vision paper, the October 2026 staleness preprint and new open-source projects all appeared within about a year. A verified preprint in spring 2027, posted after vendor disclosure and with every reference checked, establishes priority without exposing the authors to the 2026 arXiv sanctions.
